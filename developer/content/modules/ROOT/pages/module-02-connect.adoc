= Module 2: Connect an app to an issuer
:navtitle: 2. Connect an app to an issuer

*Time:* 25 minutes +
*Before you start:* Module 1 +
*By the end, you can:* say which values each kind of application needs from Keycloak; find them in runtime configuration; recognize an issuer mismatch from its error and fix it

== Why it matters

Connecting an application to Keycloak takes very few values. From the issuer,
an application finds every endpoint and key through the discovery document
you opened in Module 1. What it needs depends on its role:

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Role |Values
|Signs users in (SPA, BFF, proxy) |Issuer, client ID, redirect address, and for a confidential client its secret.
|Accepts tokens (Orders, Inventory, Notifications) |Issuer, and the audience it expects (its own name, usually fixed in code).
|Calls other services as itself (Orders) |Issuer, client ID, and secret.
|===

These values change per environment, so they live in configuration, not in
code. Because one value drives everything, the most common integration bug is
an issuer that is almost right. Operations hands you the issuer; getting it
into your configuration exactly is your job.

== Do: read the prepared configuration

. Open the link:{openshift_console_url}[OpenShift Console,window=_blank] in a
  new browser tab. On the page titled **Sign in to sso**, sign in with your
  username and password. If a welcome tour opens, click **Skip tour**.
. Select your project `{attendee_namespace}` and open **Workloads →
  ConfigMaps → frontend-runtime-config**. Its `config.json` holds `issuer` and
  `spaClientId`, the two Keycloak values the SPA reads at runtime. The SPA
  builds its redirect address from its own address. A public client has no
  secret, so there is none here.
. Open `bff-config` and find `OIDC_ISSUER`. The BFF's client ID is in its
  code; its secret is in the `bff-client` Secret, which operations filled in.
  Do not open the Secret's value.
. Open `orders-api-config`. It has two issuer values: `ISSUER` for checking
  incoming tokens and `WORKSHOP_ISSUER` for requesting its own tokens.

== Break it: the BFF with an issuer that is almost right

. Open **Workloads → ConfigMaps → bff-config → Actions → Edit ConfigMap**. It
  opens an editor for YAML (YAML Ain't Markup Language), a plain-text
  configuration format. Under `data:`, find `OIDC_ISSUER`. Edit only that
  line: `OIDC_ISSUER` also appears higher up in the `managedFields` metadata,
  which you leave alone.
. Add a trailing slash to the value, a common copy-and-paste slip, so it reads
  `pass:a[{issuer}/]`. Click **Save**.
. Open **Workloads → Deployments → bff** and choose **Restart rollout** from
  the **Actions** menu at the top right of the page.
. **Predict:** the issuer is one character off. Will the BFF start?
. Wait a minute, then open **Observe → Logs**, search for
  `did not match the requested issuer`, and click **Run Query** (click it again
  if the results do not change). The newest line shows only
  `Application run failed`; the next is long, with the part that matters at its
  end:
+
[subs="attributes+"]
----
The Issuer "{issuer}" provided in the configuration metadata did not match
the requested issuer "{issuer}/"
----
+
The BFF compared the issuer in the discovery document with the one it was
given and stopped, rather than trust tokens from an issuer it cannot match
exactly.
. Edit `bff-config` again and remove the trailing slash so `OIDC_ISSUER` is
  exactly `pass:a[{issuer}]`. The editor may now show the value in single
  quotes (`+'...'+`); keep the quotes and change only the text between them.
  Save, and restart the `bff` rollout.

== Break it: the SPA pointed at another realm

. Edit `frontend-runtime-config`. In `config.json`, change the end of `issuer`
  from `/realms/{realm_name}` to `/realms/master`, and save. `master` is a real
  realm on the same Keycloak server, but not yours.
. Restart the `frontend` rollout and wait until it shows **1 of 1 pods**.
. **Predict:** what will happen when you sign in to the SPA? Write it down,
  then reload the link:{spa_url}[SPA,window=_blank] page and click **Sign in**.
+
.What happened
[%collapsible]
====
The popup shows Keycloak's error page, **Client not found**. The issuer names
the realm, and each realm has its own clients: `master` has no `spa`. An API
given this issuer would fail differently. It would reject every token from
your realm with 401, because their `iss` and signing keys do not match.
====
. Close the popup. Edit `frontend-runtime-config` back to your issuer,
  `pass:a[{issuer}]`, save, and restart the `frontend` rollout.

== Check

. Wait until `bff` and `frontend` show **1 of 1 pods**, and check that each
  pod's **Created** time under **Workloads → Pods** is just now.
. Open `pass:a[{spa_url}/config.json]` in a new tab. It shows your issuer and
  `spa`, and no secret.
. The three apps share one Keycloak sign-in in your browser, so the SPA may
  still be signed in as `manager` from Module 1. Sign out of each app before
  you switch users, starting with the SPA now. If **Sign out** shows
  Keycloak's "Do you want to log out?" page, click **Logout**. After you sign
  out of the proxy, close any tab left on Keycloak's sign-in page.
. Sign in as `reader` through each app, then sign out: the
  link:{spa_url}[SPA,window=_blank] (a popup),
  link:{bff_url}[BFF,window=_blank] (the page goes to Keycloak and back), and
  link:{proxy_url}[proxy,window=_blank] (Keycloak appears immediately; the
  whole app is behind the sign-in). All three patterns sign in.

== Explain

. Why did the BFF refuse to start, rather than start and reject tokens later?
+
.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Spring Security checks the discovery document's `issuer` against the
configured one at startup. Failing fast turns a configuration mistake into an
obvious startup error. An API that fails only per request, like Orders,
returns 401 to every caller instead, which is harder to trace back to
configuration.
====
. Which of these values could safely be committed to Git: the issuer, the
  client ID, the redirect address, the client secret?
+
.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
The issuer, client ID, and redirect address are not secrets; they appear in
every browser sign-in. The client secret is. It belongs in a Secret or another
secret store, injected at runtime, and rotated if it leaks.
====

== At your desk

The BFF's whole Keycloak connection is its Spring Boot `application.yml`:

[source,yaml]
----
spring.security.oauth2.client:
  registration:
    keycloak:
      client-id: bff
      client-secret: ${BFF_CLIENT_SECRET}
      authorization-grant-type: authorization_code
      client-settings:
        require-proof-key: true          # PKCE
      scope: openid,profile,email
      redirect-uri: "{baseUrl}/login/oauth2/code/{registrationId}"
  provider:
    keycloak:
      issuer-uri: ${OIDC_ISSUER}         # everything else comes from discovery
----

When an integration fails, check the issuer first, character by character:
scheme, host, realm, and no trailing slash.
